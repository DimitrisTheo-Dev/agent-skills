# Choose the stronger interface

## Carry the user's taste forward
Use stated preferences as evidence: native-feeling control, deliberate density, expressive but
coherent visuals, readable hierarchy and purposeful motion can coexist. Preserve concrete
accepted/rejected examples and the reasons in the project's design contract. Do not infer a favorite
palette from an inspiration screenshot. Do not require a new taste questionnaire for each task.
Record the exact region, the accepted/rejected relationship, the reason and the scope of the
preference. An agent review does not establish owner approval. If later feedback rejects a reviewed
design, reopen that finding and carry the correction into the next render; do not defend it with the
earlier score or reviewer agreement.

## Make alternatives comparable
When the direction is open, use the smallest comparison that can settle it. A substantial identity
decision may benefit from two or three working compositions. Use comparable content and constraints,
and vary information order, spatial organization, visual voice or interaction. Inspect the widths,
content density and state transitions that could change the choice. Preserve the approved direction
where it serves the task.

Do not give one direction richer content or a more complete implementation and call that a style
preference. Keep scope comparable. A static image can compare composition; it cannot establish
interaction quality.

For a distinctive identity brief, apply the [creative exploration and rejection
protocol](creative-rejection.md). Test the direction with a realistic busy view or difficult content
as well as its most expressive view.

## Choose by observed relationships
Inspect before writing the recommendation:

1. First look: what is this, what matters and what can I do? Check the actual initial viewport,
   including loading/empty context where relevant.
2. Task access: what must I scroll past, understand or open to do the main job? Large introductory
   text must earn its footprint. Preserve needed explanation; do not optimize every interface into a
   dense table.
3. Scanning: can I compare realistic items and distinguish hierarchy? Check repeated rows, long
   names, labels, values and actions. Fix copy length and organization before shrinking the type.
4. Composition: does visual weight, space, type, imagery and material form an intentional whole at
   both widths? Inspect shared anchors and optical alignment.
5. Character: what makes this specific to the product beyond its logo or color? What does it share
   with the supplied quality references, and what does it deliberately do differently?
6. Feel: perform the transition and its reversal. Check feedback, focus, continuity and input
   readiness. A striking still cannot outweigh broken use.

Choose the strongest combination of task clarity, coherent craft and product character. Do not
average away a serious failure with a high aesthetic score. If each alternative has a major
weakness, repair the strongest composition or build a meaningfully different one. Do not select
solely because it is familiar, faster to code or easier to describe.

## Preserve a concise decision
Record the selected direction, one visible reason it serves the task, one visible reason it has
character, the rejected tradeoff and remaining uncertainty. Keep tokens in code and link to
governing components. Capture the alternatives before retiring disposable code. Continue reversible
work autonomously; ask only where consequential missing priorities change the product.

## Build the complete first flow
Resolve layout, type, copy, relevant states and feedback as the first complete flow takes shape. Use
suitable existing components and dependencies. When time is limited, protect the core flow and
reduce optional feature scope.

Use ui-quality-gate for the full inspection and correction loop. A comparison may reveal a weakness
without justifying a universal rule. Keep product-specific lessons scoped and update the source of a
repeated defect.
