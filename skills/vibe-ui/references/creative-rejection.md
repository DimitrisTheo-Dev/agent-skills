# Creative exploration and rejection

Use for **Shape** work with an explicit differentiation, brand identity or ambitious redesign brief. This is not a universal originality quota for operational interfaces, established brand systems or narrow repairs.

## Before composing

Write an evidence inventory: (1) who the visitor is, (2) their immediate decision, (3) the product's actual mechanism or output, (4) recognizable owned assets such as an avatar or product UI, (5) proof available for publication, and (6) the requested emotional register and known user rejections. Mark unknowns and do not invent proof.

State one creative tension in concrete terms, such as “human conversation shown through a rigorous hiring review.” Name one constraint that protects the task: for example, the visitor must identify the product and CTA without watching an animation.

## Explore truly different concepts

Render **two or three** concepts with identical real copy, core features, CTA, and evidence. Different concepts should make different decisions about **at least two meaningful axes**, such as:

- The opening narrative: lead with a person, an interaction, a transformation or an outcome.
- The product artifact: actual avatar interview, review output, workflow diagram or real application surface.
- The composition: immersive scene, editorial sequence, split interaction or other justified information architecture.
- The interaction: directly try a supported task, inspect a real output, compare perspectives or watch an honest recorded example.

A new font, palette, background texture, border radius, centered/split alignment, or card rearrangement **alone is not a distinct concept**. Do not manufacture difference by giving one variant weaker copy or less proof.

For a meaningful product asset, keep a clear view of it in at least one concept; if a concept intentionally omits it, document the reason and verify that comprehension or character actually improves.

## Review the renders, not the pitches

At wide and narrow widths, cover the logo and ask: “Can I identify this offering from the experience and visual? Does an adjacent competitor gain this design essentially unchanged by switching words and colors?” This is a diagnostic thought experiment, not a proof of worldwide originality.

Inspect:
1. First-view comprehension: product, audience, value and actual next action.
2. Product evidence: can a visitor point to the real mechanism or output, not just decorative art?
3. Composition: visible relationship between headline, product and CTA; deliberate full-viewport rhythm.
4. Identity: an existing signature asset or ownable representation that survives removing gradient effects.
5. Repetition: ordinary interactions remain usable, without spectacle or forced motion.
6. Honesty: authentic footage/screens vs conceptual visuals are unmistakably distinguished.

Record **one visible win, one failure and one specific rejected tradeoff** per concept. If both concepts still look like interchangeable SaaS templates, change the narrative or product representation instead of selecting the one with prettier textures.

## Stop conditions

- **Reject:** A generic concept presented as distinctive, a product displaced by anonymous art, a broken comprehension/action path, or a mocked capability represented as live.
- **Revise:** The concept is distinctive but visually unbalanced, unreadable, slow or dependent on an unsupported asset.
- **Accept for full build:** A coherent concept advances the actual task, protects trust and shows product-specific identity at both widths.

No literal formula, predetermined number of colors or external reference can guarantee taste. If the brief needs restraint and the ordinary interface already expresses the brand, preserve it. Record unavailable renders as **unverified** rather than describing them as tested.
