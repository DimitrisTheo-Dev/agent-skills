# Validate the experience

## Structure outline

Before polishing surfaces, name the page's job and write the actual reading/action order. Identify what belongs in the first useful viewport, what can follow and what can be deferred. Avoid a hero that pushes daily work out of view or an onboarding checklist that obscures the first outcome.

Define the actual entities and their labels. A collection item, section, status and action need different treatments. Keep terminology consistent across navigation, headings, buttons, results and errors. Explain what a button will do with a concrete verb and object when useful.

## Compare candidates fairly

Use identical realistic content, equivalent feature completeness and relevant states. Include at least one difficult item and an ordinary populated view. Compare:

- Recognition: can the intended person distinguish objects and locate a known item?
- Decision effort: are the right attributes visible together?
- Action effort: what input, navigation and repeated work does completion require?
- Orientation: is current context clear when opening details, moving back or changing steps?
- Confidence: can the person understand the result before a consequential commitment?
- Character: does the representation express this product through its real content?

These are inspection questions. Do not invent usability timings, conversion gains or user preferences. Mark a predicted benefit as a hypothesis until observed.

## Required branches by pattern

A collection needs populated, empty, loading, failed, filtered/no-result and selection states where applicable. Include realistic volume, missing imagery, long names, duplicate titles and recovery. Keep data truthful.

A staged form needs understandable progress, preserved values on back, editable review, local validation, final submission feedback and a way out. Test browser back and refresh only to the extent persistence is promised. Do not invent persistence. A one-page form needs the same honesty and recovery without artificial steps.

A preview needs a clear relationship to inputs, accurate scope, loading/failure distinction if asynchronous and a narrow layout where it does not obstruct the active input. Preserve the difference between preview, draft and published state.

An overlay needs focus management, dismissal appropriate to its risk, return to the trigger and usable scrolling. A panel must not trap work that belongs in a full page. Navigation should remain understandable without motion.

## Small-screen experience

Prioritize one main work region. Preserve decision-critical metadata and meaningful controls; move secondary detail access deliberately. Keep important information available rather than hiding it solely to fit. Inspect the on-screen keyboard, sticky actions, safe areas and long translated labels when relevant.

Use a whole-flow walkthrough and capture key states. A screenshot can support hierarchy findings but cannot verify recovery or task completion. Stop expanding alternatives when the decision is supported; continue fixing observed major problems within scope.
