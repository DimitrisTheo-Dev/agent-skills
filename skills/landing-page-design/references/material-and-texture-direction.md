# Landing page material and texture direction

Use this reference **only when visual identity, hero art, background material or surface treatment is an open decision**. These are ten optional visual languages to investigate, not a template, trend forecast, approval list or instruction to add ten effects. A landing page may need none of them. Product clarity, actual proof, accessibility and a usable next action remain more important.

## Start with what the product already owns

Before adding abstract art, inventory the actual product screens, avatars, mascots, photography, illustrations, logos and distinctive interaction patterns. Identify the visual that makes the product recognizable with the brand name hidden.

**Preserve meaningful brand assets when redesigning the surrounding aesthetic.** For example, if an AI interviewer has a recognizable avatar, keep the authentic avatar in a relevant interview or demonstration moment; use a gradient or texture as atmosphere around it, rather than substituting a decorative sculpture for the product. Do not misrepresent a mockup as a functioning interview.

Choose the job for any proposed treatment: **explain a mechanism, make the product recognizable, establish a specific atmosphere, or clarify depth and interaction**. If the treatment has no job, remove it.

## Ten material directions to explore

Treat the following as **art-direction prompts**, not CSS recipes or default brand choices. Each entry names a useful condition and the failure to watch for.

| Treatment | What it can communicate / where it earns space | Common failure / how to constrain it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Thermal gradients** | A deliberate warm-to-cool field suggests intensity, change or energy. Useful behind a transformation or a visually dramatic brand moment. | Arbitrary heatmap colors can imply nonexistent measurements. Keep it atmospheric unless tied to real data; reserve a calm area for text and verify contrast. |
| **2. Blueprint grids** | Precise measured lines can support engineering, spatial planning, an architecture diagram, or a building-in-public identity. | A page-wide grid becomes generic developer-tool wallpaper. Use a purposeful scale, crop and hierarchy; keep grids out of dense reading areas. |
| **3. Water ripples** | Concentric waves can express response, voice, presence, touch or cause-and-effect in one focal interaction. | Endless pulsing suggests fake live activity and distracts from reading. Trigger motion from a real or clearly labeled simulated event; provide a static and reduced-motion state. |
| **4. Speckled graphite** | Fine neutral grain gives a dark surface physical weight, restraint and editorial character. Useful as a quiet frame for high-fidelity product imagery. | Heavy noise muddies type, banding and gradients, and can inflate media size. Keep grain subtle, behind content, with clean panels for small text. |
| **5. Fluted glass** | Ribbed/refraction-like material can introduce controlled depth, separation or a distinctive transition between content and backdrop. | Large blurred glass panels reduce legibility and can be expensive to render. Use on a small nonessential region; never refract essential copy or controls. |
| **6. Contour lines** | Topographic isolines suggest terrain, mapping, systems, layers or a measured journey. They can guide the eye through a process or visualization. | Random topographic decoration looks like a stock tech background. Align contours to a real concept; avoid implying data accuracy if they are illustrative. |
| **7. Grainy gradient** | A smooth color field with fine film grain or dithering creates material richness without a glossy, oversaturated glow. Strong for a single hero art direction. | Overdone saturation, multi-hue text and repeated gradients make the page anonymous. Keep palette constrained, emphasize one area, and test mobile crops. |
| **8. Single-hued landscapes** | A monochromatic natural, architectural or imagined environment can give the brand scale, focus and distinctive atmospheric depth. | Generic mountains, deserts or horizons are irrelevant to many products. Choose a scene connected to the customer's world; separate genuine evidence from conceptual art. |
| **9. Grass texture** | A close, tactile organic surface can connect to land, nature, sport, outdoor work, agriculture or a deliberately earthy brand. | Unmotivated grass behind enterprise software is decoration, not differentiation. Use authentic source material, thoughtful crop and restrained placement; never sacrifice legibility. |
| **10. Soft shadow** | A consistent, believable light source establishes elevation, touchability and focus around actual controls or a meaningful physical object. | Giant blurry shadows and a floating-card collection feel artificially generated. Use few elevation levels, correct contact/ambient shadow proportions and stable hover/focus behavior. |

A material direction is **not** a reason to invent icons, feature cards, metrics, fake testimonials, unsupported interaction states, product imagery or fictitious data.

## Selection and execution procedure

1. **State the product's world.** In one sentence explain why this material belongs to the product's people, objects, mechanism or identity. If the same explanation works for five unrelated products, the direction is too generic.
2. **Preserve the hero's job.** The offer, intended visitor, outcome, primary CTA and recognizable product or result must remain understandable in roughly five seconds. The existing avatar, screenshot or product artifact should not vanish simply because a background became more artistic.
3. **Compare at most two materially different treatments** on identical real content, including desktop and narrow mobile. One candidate may deliberately have no texture. Compare first-view clarity, identity with the logo removed, attention distribution and performance cost.
4. **Concentrate the craft.** Use one primary treatment in one memorable area, with a possible quiet supporting material. Keep navigation, forms, product panels and factual proof calmer. Do not apply every pattern in the table.
5. **Specify the build.** Prefer a static optimized image or economical CSS for visual-only texture. Use canvas/WebGL/shaders only when justified by a real interaction and budget, and provide a still fallback. Avoid full-screen animated filters when a still communicates the same point.
6. **Inspect the artifact.** Verify text/CTA contrast, crop, responsive order, readable text at enlargement, reduced motion, keyboard focus and the actual load experience. Check that important visuals and labels survive with the background disabled or media unavailable. Record assumptions and real test evidence separately.

## Review red flags

Reject a treatment or reconsider the composition when: the product is harder to identify; the avatar/real UI has been replaced by anonymous abstract art; text sits over uncontrolled contrast; motion loops without user meaning; the same texture fills hero, features and footer; a decorative grid competes with the product; a mobile crop hides the subject; or the result could be relabeled as an unrelated SaaS startup without changing anything substantial.
